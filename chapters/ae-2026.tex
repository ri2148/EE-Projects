\item 
If a matrix can be written as $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{uv^T}$, where both $u$ and $v$ are $n$-dimensional real-valued non-zero column vectors, then the rank of the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is \underline{\hspace{1cm}}.
\hfill (GATE~AE~2026)\\

\solution 


    \noindent
    The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is formed by taking the outer product of two $n \times 1$ non-zero column vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$:
    \begin{align}
    \mathbf{A = uv^T}
    \end{align}
    Writing $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v^T}$ explicitly:
    \begin{align}
    \mathbf{u}= \myvec{ u_1 \\ u_2 \\ \vdots \\ u_n }, \quad
    \mathbf{v^T} = \myvec{ v_1 & v_2 & \dots & v_n }
    \end{align}

    \noindent
    Multiplying $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v^T}$ gives the $n \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{A}$:
    \begin{align}
    \mathbf{A} = \myvec{ 
    u_1 v_1 & u_1 v_2 & \dots & u_1 v_n \\ 
    u_2 v_1 & u_2 v_2 & \dots & u_2 v_n \\ 
    \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 
    u_n v_1 & u_n v_2 & \dots & u_n v_n 
    }
    \end{align}

    \noindent 
    Notice that each column of $\mathbf{A}$ can be written as a scalar multiple of vector $\mathbf{u}$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 1st column: $v_1 \mathbf{u}$
        \item 2nd column: $v_2 \mathbf{u}$
        \item $j^{\text{th}}$ column: $v_j \mathbf{u}$
    \end{itemize}
    

    \noindent 
The rank of a matrix is equal to the number of non zero rows in its echeleon form. When we reduce the above matrix into the echeleon form, we notice that each row is a multiple of another as shown above. Therefore, when we apply elementary row transformations by multiplying the rows with the necessary constants and subtracting, all rows but one become non zero. Hence, the rank of the matrix becomes $1$. \\ \\
\noindent Example: \\
Consider $3$-dimensional non-zero vectors $\mathbf{u} = \myvec{1 \\ 2 \\ 3}$ and $\mathbf{v} = \myvec{1 \\ 4 \\ 2}$.

\noindent
The matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is formed as:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{uv^T} &= \myvec{1 \\ 2 \\ 3} \myvec{1 & 4 & 2} \\
&= \myvec{
1 & 4 & 2 \\
2 & 8 & 4 \\
3 & 12 & 6
}
\end{align}

\noindent
Applying elementary row transformations step-by-step:

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent
Eliminate the non-zero entry in the second row using $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{A} \xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1} \myvec{
1 & 4 & 2 \\
0 & 0 & 0 \\
3 & 12 & 6
}
\end{align}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent
Eliminate the non-zero entry in the third row using $R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_1$:
\begin{align}
\xrightarrow{R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_1} \myvec{
1 & 4 & 2 \\
0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
}
\end{align}

\noindent
The matrix is now in row echelon form with only $1$ non-zero row. Hence, $\text{rank}\brak{\mathbf{A}} = 1$.\\

Any conic section in a 2D plane \brak{p, q} is expressed by the general equation:
\begin{align}
S\brak{p, q} = ap^2 + 2hpq + bq^2 + 2gp + 2fq + c = 0
\end{align}

\begin{align}
r = \frac{p_{\text{orb}}}{1 + e \cos\theta}
\end{align}

\begin{align}

\end{align}

\begin{align}
p^2 + q^2 = p_{\text{orb}}^2 - 2e p_{\text{orb}} p + e^2 p^2
\end{align}
Rearranging into standard form $S\brak{p, q} = 0$:
\begin{align}
\brak{1 - e^2}p^2 + 0 \cdot pq + 1 \cdot q^2 + 2\brak{e p_{\text{orb}}}p + 0 \cdot q - p_{\text{orb}}^2 = 0
\end{align}

\begin{align}
a = 1 - e^2, \quad b = 1, \quad c = -p_{\text{orb}}^2, \quad h = 0, \quad g = e p_{\text{orb}}, \quad f = 0
\end{align}

\begin{align}
M = \myvec{a & h & g \\ h & b & f \\ g & f & c} = \myvec{1 - e^2 & 0 & e p_{\text{orb}} \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ e p_{\text{orb}} & 0 & -p_{\text{orb}}^2}
\end{align}

\begin{align}
\Delta = \mydet{M} = 1 \cdot \mydet{1 - e^2 & e p_{\text{orb}} \\ e p_{\text{orb}} & -p_{\text{orb}}^2} = -p_{\text{orb}}^2\brak{1 - e^2} - e^2 p_{\text{orb}}^2 = -p_{\text{orb}}^2
\end{align}
Since $p_{\text{orb}} \neq 0$, $\Delta \neq 0$. The curve is strictly a non-degenerate conic.\\

The principal 2$\times$2 submatrix $A = \myvec{a & h \\ h & b}$ gives:
\begin{align}

\end{align}

\end{align}
\begin{align}
\mydet{A}=1-e^2=1-1.3795=-0.3795

\noindent A quadratic form whose matrix has eigenvalues of mixed signs is defined as indefinite \brak{\text{it can take both positive and negative values depending on the direction vector}}.\\

Substituting $\lambda_2 = -|\lambda_2|$ \brak{\text{since } \lambda_2 < 0} yields:

Completing the square and shifting the origin brings the equation into the familiar standard form of a hyperbola:

\noindent Hence, it can be said that the trajectory of the satellite is a hyperbola.

\begin{align}
\myvec{ a & b } \myvec{ 1 \\ 1 } &= k = \myvec{ c & d } \myvec{ 1 \\ 1 } \\
\implies \myvec{ a & b \\ c & d } \myvec{ 1 \\ 1 } &= \myvec{ k \\ k } \\
\implies \myvec{ a & b \\ c & d } \myvec{ 1 \\ 1 } &= k \myvec{ 1 \\ 1 }
\end{align}

\noindent $\implies \myvec{ 1 \\ 1 }$ is an eigenvector and $k = a + b$ is an eigenvalue.

a - c = d - b = -\brak{b - d} = k'
\end{align}

\begin{align}
\myvec{ a & b \\ c & d } \myvec{ 1 \\ -1 } &= \myvec{ a - b \\ c - d } = \myvec{ k' \\ -k' } = k' \myvec{ 1 \\ -1 }
\end{align}

\noindent $\implies \myvec{ 1 \\ -1 }$ is an eigenvector and $d - b$ is the $2^{\text{nd}}$ eigenvalue.

\item 
Find the minimum value of the function $f\brak{x} = \abs{x} + \abs{2x+3}$ for real $x$. \hfill (GATE~AE~2026)\\ \\
\solution \\ 

\noindent The critical points occur where the expressions inside the absolute values equal zero:
\begin{align}
x &= 0 \\[0.5ex]
2x + 3 = 0 &\implies x = -\frac{3}{2} = -1.5
\end{align}

\noindent These two critical points divide the real line into three sub-intervals:
\begin{align}
\brak{-\infty, -1.5}, \quad [-1.5, 0), \quad \text{and} \quad [0, \infty)
\end{align}


\begin{itemize}
    \item For $x < -1.5$: \\
    Both expressions inside the absolute values are negative, so $\abs{x} = -x$ and $\abs{2x+3} = -\brak{2x+3}$:
    \begin{align}
    f\brak{x} = -x - \brak{2x + 3} = -3x - 3
    \end{align}

    \item For $-1.5 \le x < 0$: \\
    The term $x$ is negative ($\abs{x} = -x$) while $2x+3$ is non-negative ($\abs{2x+3} = 2x+3$):
    \begin{align}
    f\brak{x} = -x + \brak{2x + 3} = x + 3
    \end{align}

    \item For $x \ge 0$: \\
    Both expressions are non-negative, so $\abs{x} = x$ and $\abs{2x+3} = 2x+3$:
    \begin{align}
    f\brak{x} = x + \brak{2x + 3} = 3x + 3
    \end{align}
\end{itemize}

Combining these cases gives the complete piecewise definition:
\begin{align}
f\brak{x} = \begin{cases} 
-3x - 3, & \text{if } x < -1.5 \\
x + 3, & \text{if } -1.5 \le x < 0 \\
3x + 3, & \text{if } x \ge 0 
\end{cases}
\end{align}

\noindent Analyzing the behavior of each linear segment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item On $\brak{-\infty, -1.5}$, the line $f\brak{x} = -3x - 3$ has a negative slope ($-3$), so $f\brak{x}$ strictly decreases as $x$ approaches $-1.5$.
    \item On $[-1.5, 0)$, the line $f\brak{x} = x + 3$ has a positive slope ($+1$), so $f\brak{x}$ strictly increases as $x$ moves away from $-1.5$.
    \item On $[0, \infty)$, the line $f\brak{x} = 3x + 3$ has a positive slope ($+3$), so $f\brak{x}$ continues to increase.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Since $f\brak{x}$ decreases for $x < -1.5$ and increases for $x > -1.5$, the minimum value occurs at $x = -1.5$:
\begin{align}
f\brak{-1.5} = -1.5 + 3 = 1.5
\end{align}

E &= 200 \times 10^9 \text{ N/m}^2 \\

k_{\text{bar}} = \frac{10^{-4} \times 200 \times 10^9}{0.1} = 2 \times 10^8 \text{ N/m}

\frac{1}{k_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{k_{\text{bar}}} + \frac{1}{k}

\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k_{\text{eq}}}{M}} = \sqrt{\frac{10^8}{100}} = \sqrt{10^6} = 1000 \text{ rad/s}

M \frac{d^2 x}{d t^2} &= M g - k_{\text{eq}}(\delta_{\text{st}} + x) \\
M \frac{d^2 x}{d t^2} &= M g - k_{\text{eq}}\delta_{\text{st}} - k_{\text{eq}}x

M \frac{d^2 x}{d t^2} &= (k_{\text{eq}}\delta_{\text{st}}) - k_{\text{eq}}\delta_{\text{st}} - k_{\text{eq}}x

M \frac{d^2 x}{d t^2} &= -k_{\text{eq}}x
